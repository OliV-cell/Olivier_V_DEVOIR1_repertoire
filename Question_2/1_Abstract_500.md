**Shorten Abstract:**

Scientific retractions have risen, raising questions about why. It is unclear whether this increase reflects a higher volume of flawed articles or better detection and removal of problematic papers. To explore this, we analyzed 2,047 retracted articles from PubMed, focusing on trends in the time interval between publication and retraction. The average time-to-retraction across all articles was 32.91 months. However, articles published before 2002 took significantly longer to retract, suggesting that journals are now retracting articles more quickly.

To assess the role of increased scrutiny, we examined the relationship between time-to-retraction and journal impact factor (IF). While high-IF journals retract articles more quickly, this factor explained only 1% of the variance. The correlation was stronger for articles retracted due to misconduct, but still modest overall. Therefore, greater scrutiny in prominent journals does not explain the observed increase.

Our analysis also revealed shifts in the causes for retraction. Retractions for previously rare offences have become more frequent, indicating that the grounds for retraction have expanded, such as plagiarism.

Authors with a single retraction have become more prominent. They wrote 63.1% of retracted papers from 1993 to 2012, up from 46% in 1972–1992. Authors with multiple retractions were more influential earlier but accounted for less of the recent increase. This shift suggests retractions are now spread more broadly across the scientific community, rather than concentrated among a few prolific offenders.

In conclusion, the rise in retractions reflects changes in the behavior of both authors and institutions.
